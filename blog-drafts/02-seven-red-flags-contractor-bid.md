## 1. The Mystery "Allowance"

This is the most common one, and it's the one that turns a $68,000 remodel into a $94,000 remodel halfway through.

An "allowance" is a placeholder dollar amount in the bid for an item that hasn't been spec'd yet. Common allowances: tile, plumbing fixtures, lighting, countertops, hardware.

The allowance number itself isn't the red flag. **The red flag is when the allowance is vague, low, or undefined.**

Look for lines like:
- "Tile allowance: $8/sqft" *(but doesn't say how many sqft)*
- "Plumbing fixtures allowance: $1,500" *(for a full bath — that buys budget fixtures only)*
- "Lighting allowance: TBD per homeowner selection"

What this means in practice: the contractor has bid the labor and overhead on the assumption that you'll pick the cheapest option in every category. The moment you walk into the showroom and pick anything mid-range, you're paying the upcharge plus the contractor's markup on top.

**How to fix it:** Demand specifics. *"For a 50 sqft kitchen backsplash with porcelain subway tile from Home Depot's mid-range line, what's the all-in cost?"* If they can't give you a number, they shouldn't be giving you a bid.

## 2. "General Conditions" Above 10%

Every legit contractor has overhead — project management time, supervision, trash haul, port-a-john rental, temporary fencing, dumpster fees, the truck running back and forth. Industry standard for "general conditions" or "GC fee" on a residential remodel: **5–8% of the project total**, sometimes nudging to 10% on small or complex jobs.

If your bid lists general conditions at **12–20%**, something is being double-billed. The contractor is either:
- Counting supervision time inside *both* general conditions *and* labor (paying themselves twice)
- Loading their profit margin into GC and labeling it as overhead
- Padding because they expect you not to notice

**How to fix it:** Ask, in writing: *"What does the general conditions line cover specifically? Can you provide a breakdown?"* A reputable contractor will hand you a one-page breakdown. A padder will get defensive.

## 3. No Payment Schedule (or 50% Upfront)

A fair payment schedule for a $60K–$120K remodel looks something like this:

- **10% deposit** at contract signing
- **20%** at material delivery
- **20%** at rough-in completion (electrical, plumbing, framing)
- **20%** at drywall completion
- **20%** at substantial completion
- **10% final** at punch-list completion + lien releases

The red flag: **anything that asks for 30%+ upfront before work starts.** A 50% deposit is a financial structure that protects the contractor from cash-flow problems — at *your* expense. If they need half your money before swinging a hammer, they're undercapitalized, and you're the one absorbing their risk.

The other red flag: **no schedule at all.** "Payments as work progresses" is not a payment schedule. It's a blank check.

**How to fix it:** Insist on a written milestone-based schedule with specific dollar amounts tied to specific completion criteria. If they refuse, walk away.

## 4. The "Verbal Will Match" Clause

You'll spot this one in conversation more than in writing. You ask about something — maybe a window upgrade, a specific outlet location, a tile pattern — and the contractor says some version of:

> *"Don't worry about that, we'll take care of it on site."*
> *"That's included, we'll match it to whatever you decide."*
> *"We can sort that detail out during construction."*

**Translation: it's not in the bid.** When the time comes, it'll be a change order. And change orders are where the contractor's hourly rate goes from $85 to $145, because now they have all the leverage and you have none.

**How to fix it:** Every detail discussed gets a line item in the bid or a written note in the scope of work. If it's not on paper, it's not in the project.

## 5. No Itemized Materials List

A bid that says "all materials included" with one lump-sum number is hiding something. Maybe the markup is 40% instead of 15%. Maybe the cabinets are the bottom-tier grade. Maybe the "Kohler" faucet is actually a Kohler *Coralais* — the budget line — when you assumed it was a Purist.

A good contractor's bid includes:
- Specific brand and model number for every appliance
- Cabinet manufacturer + line name (e.g., "KraftMaid Vantage, Birch, Maple stain")
- Countertop material, slab thickness, edge profile
- Tile manufacturer + SKU
- Plumbing fixture brand + model
- Light fixture brand + model

If your bid doesn't have this level of detail, request it. If they say "we'll figure out brands during selection meetings," that means they want flexibility to swap to whatever they have a margin deal on with their supplier — which means **they** decide what you get.

**How to fix it:** Reply with: *"Can you provide the bid with specific brand and model numbers for all materials? I want to compare options before signing."* This single sentence saves homeowners thousands.

## 6. The Sub-Contractor Shuffle

The person who walks through your house with a notepad, hand-shakes you, and emails the bid is often *not* the person who's going to do the work. That's normal — most general contractors run a network of subs (plumbing, electrical, tile, drywall).

The red flag is when **the GC won't tell you who their subs are**.

You should know, before signing:
- Who is doing the electrical work? Is the license current?
- Who is doing the plumbing? Same question.
- Who is the tile sub? Have they done your tile before?
- Are the subs W-2 employees of the GC, or independent contractors hired per-job?

This matters because the GC's reputation is only as good as the subs they show up with. And in a hot market, the best subs are booked solid — so the lower-cost GCs are sometimes pulling in whoever is available that week.

**How to fix it:** Ask for the names of the three primary subs. Look up their licenses on your state's licensing board (free, takes 2 minutes per name). Read reviews. If the GC won't share, that's your answer.

## 7. The Timeline That's Too Good to Be True

A full mid-range kitchen remodel — start to finish, including permits, demo, rough-in, inspection, finish work, and punch list — takes **6 to 10 weeks** for an experienced crew working efficiently. Bathroom: 3 to 5 weeks. Basement finish: 8 to 14 weeks.

Red flag: a contractor promising **half** that timeline.

There are two reasons a contractor promises an unrealistic timeline:
1. **They're trying to win the bid.** They know the homeowner wants the kitchen done before Thanksgiving. They promise it. They figure they'll deal with the slip later.
2. **They're planning to cut corners.** Pulling permits takes time. Letting drywall mud cure takes time. Letting tile mortar set takes time. A timeline that ignores these steps means they're not doing them properly.

**How to fix it:** Ask for a written schedule with named milestones and dates. *"Demo complete by X. Rough-in complete by Y. Inspection scheduled for Z. Cabinets installed by A. Substantial completion by B."* Now you have something to hold them to — and you can spot which step they're planning to skip.

---

## The Pattern Behind All Seven

Notice that every red flag on this list comes back to the same thing: **vagueness.** Vague allowances. Vague overhead. Vague payment milestones. Vague materials. Vague subs. Vague timelines.

Contractors who plan to do good work give specific answers. Contractors who plan to make it up as they go give vague ones.

You don't need to be a construction expert to protect yourself. You just need to ask: *"Can you put that in writing with specifics?"* at every point on this list. The contractor's response will tell you everything you need to know.

---

## Bonus: The 30-Second Self-Check

Before you sign anything, ask yourself:

1. Does every dollar in this bid have a clear destination?
2. Could a stranger read this bid and know exactly what's getting built?
3. Are the materials specific enough that I could buy them myself?
4. Is the payment schedule tied to deliverables I can verify?
5. Does the timeline include named milestones?

If you can answer "yes" to all five, you have a good contractor and a clean bid. If you can't, you have a renegotiation to do — *before* you sign.
