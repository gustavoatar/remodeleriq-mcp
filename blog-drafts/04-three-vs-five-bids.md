# Should You Get 3 Bids or 5? What Homeowners Don't Realize About the Numbers

Every renovation article says *"get three bids."* It's the most common advice in home improvement. It's also incomplete — because the right number depends on the project size, your market, and a hidden cost that nobody talks about.

## The math behind "three bids"

Here's what three bids actually gives you statistically:

For a typical mid-range remodel, the three bids will cluster within about a 25% range. The highest will be ~15% above the median; the lowest will be ~10% below. Your median bid is roughly the fair price.

That's useful — for mid-range projects. But it breaks down at the edges.

## When 3 bids is right

**Project size: $5,000–$40,000.** The opportunity cost of collecting more bids exceeds the savings.

**Example:** You're getting a bathroom refresh quoted at $18K. The difference between getting 3 bids and 5 bids might save you $1,500. Each contractor needs 1.5 hours of your time to walk the site, plus 30 minutes reviewing each bid when it comes back. That's 5 extra hours of your life for a potential $1,500 — better-than-minimum-wage if it works, *worse* than minimum wage when you factor in the no-show contractors and the ones who never send the bid.

**For most homeowners under $40K, three bids is the sweet spot.**

## When 5 bids is right

**Project size: $50,000+** OR **highly variable trades:**

For larger projects, the dollar variance widens. A $90K kitchen might come in with bids ranging from $72K to $108K — a $36K spread. Getting two more bids (5 total) tightens that spread and gives you real leverage in negotiation.

Highly variable trades — meaning trades where pricing depends heavily on individual contractor experience and approach — also benefit from more bids:
- **Foundation work** (excavation strategy varies hugely)
- **Roof replacement on complex roofs** (multiple pitches, dormers, etc.)
- **Custom carpentry** (skill level drives both price and quality)
- **Specialty tile or stonework**

For these, 5 bids isn't excessive — it's risk management.

## The hidden cost nobody talks about

Every bid you collect costs the contractor money.

The walk-through is 60–90 minutes. The bid prep is 2–4 hours. The follow-up calls are another hour. A contractor who bids a $60K project spends $400–$700 in time and overhead before they know if they've won.

Reputable contractors track their **bid hit rate** — the percentage of bids that turn into signed contracts. Anything below 30% and the contractor either prices higher to compensate (you're now paying for their losing bids) or stops bidding for unserious-looking projects altogether.

**The implication for you:** quality contractors will skip your project if they sense you're treating bidding like a sport. Get more than five bids and word travels — you're "the homeowner who shops five places for everything." That's not a reputation that attracts the best work.

## The asymmetric rule: 3 quotes that matter, not 5 that don't

Here's what most homeowners get wrong: they collect 3 quotes from the first 3 contractors who answer the phone. That's 3 bids but it's not 3 *signals*.

Better strategy: source contractors deliberately, then collect 3 bids from contractors you'd actually consider hiring.

**How to source:**
1. **Two referrals** from people whose homes you've seen (and liked). The friend whose kitchen looks great is worth 50 Yelp reviews.
2. **One trade-specific source** for the dominant trade in your project. Tile-heavy bath? Ask at a tile showroom (not Home Depot — a specialty showroom). Kitchen? Ask at a cabinet showroom.
3. **One verified local license** from your state board's contractor lookup, filtered for active licenses with no recent complaints.

These three sources collectively give you a higher-quality bid set than five random contractors found via Google.

## What to do with the numbers once you have them

Three bids in hand. Now what?

### Step 1 — Throw out the lowest bid

Counterintuitive but true. The lowest bid is almost always a contractor who either (a) missed scope items they'll later change-order, or (b) is desperate for work, meaning their current jobs aren't keeping them busy, which is itself a red flag.

The lowest bid wins for paint jobs and routine repairs. It rarely wins for remodels.

### Step 2 — Throw out the highest bid

The highest bid is either a contractor pricing for the privilege of working with you (legitimate for elite contractors with a waitlist) or one pricing high on the assumption you won't push back (less legitimate).

For most homeowners, this leaves the **middle bid** as your anchor.

### Step 3 — Use the others as leverage

You don't tell contractor A what contractor B quoted. You ask contractor A to walk through their bid with you, line by line, and identify any line they'd be willing to revisit. Often they'll voluntarily come down on something — the general conditions, the project management fee, the cabinet brand they have a deal with.

## The math by project tier

| Project size | Bids to collect | Time investment | Likely savings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under $10K | 2–3 | 3–5 hours | $300–$800 |
| $10–40K | 3 | 5–8 hours | $1,500–$4,000 |
| $40–100K | 3–4 | 8–12 hours | $5,000–$12,000 |
| $100K+ | 4–5 | 12–18 hours | $10,000–$30,000 |

## The single most important advice

Whatever number of bids you collect, **make sure they're priced against the same scope.** If contractor A bid a tile backsplash and contractor B bid a glass mosaic, you're not comparing prices, you're comparing scope.

Write your own scope of work first. Hand the same one-page document to every contractor. *Then* compare.
