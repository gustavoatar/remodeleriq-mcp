# How to Read a Construction Quote: A Line-by-Line Guide for Homeowners

A contractor's quote is supposed to tell you exactly what you're buying and for what price. In practice, it tells you what the contractor *wants* you to see. The careful homeowner reads what's between the lines.

Here's the line-by-line decoder we use at [RemodelerIQ](https://remodeleriq.com) when we analyze a bid.

## The 8 sections every fair quote contains

1. **Scope of work** — what's being built, in plain language
2. **Materials list** — specific brands and model numbers
3. **Labor** — broken out by trade
4. **General conditions** — the contractor's overhead
5. **Allowances** — placeholders for items not yet specified
6. **Permits and inspections** — who's pulling, who's paying
7. **Payment schedule** — when money changes hands
8. **Exclusions and assumptions** — what's NOT included

Anything missing from this list is something the contractor doesn't want you to notice.

## Section 1 — Scope of Work

This is the project description. Bad scopes use phrases like *"refresh kitchen"* or *"update bathroom"*. Good scopes name every action: *"Demo existing cabinets and countertops. Install new semi-custom shaker cabinets (KraftMaid Vantage, Birch, Maple stain) per floor plan. Install quartz countertops (Cambria, color TBD from Tier 1 selection). Install LVP flooring throughout (Coretec Plus HD, color TBD)."*

If your scope of work fits in one paragraph for a $60K project, push back.

## Section 2 — Materials List

This is where contractor markup hides. A fair material section names:
- Brand
- Model or product line
- Color/finish
- Quantity (sqft, count, linear feet)
- Whether labor is included in the line or separate

**Red flag:** "Cabinets — semi-custom" without naming the manufacturer. The contractor preserves freedom to swap to whatever line has the highest margin for them that week.

## Section 3 — Labor (broken by trade)

Most bids lump labor into one number. A real itemized bid separates:
- Demolition labor + disposal
- Framing/structural
- Plumbing (rough-in + finish)
- Electrical (rough-in + finish + lighting install)
- Drywall + paint
- Tile install
- Cabinet install
- Finish carpentry (trim, doors, hardware)

Why this matters: when one trade runs over (and one always does), you can see exactly where. A lump-sum labor number lets the contractor blame "supply chain" for any overage.

**Sanity check ranges (mid-range remodel, 2026 national):**
- Demolition: 3–5% of total project cost
- Framing/structural: 0–8% (only if structural work)
- Plumbing: 8–14%
- Electrical: 6–12%
- Drywall/paint: 7–11%
- Tile install: 5–9%
- Cabinet install: 4–7%
- Finish carpentry: 4–8%

Total labor for a remodel without structural changes: roughly **38–45% of project cost.** Anything outside this range deserves explanation.

## Section 4 — General Conditions

This is the contractor's overhead — supervision, dumpster, port-a-john, temporary protection, weekly cleaning, project management time. Industry standard:

- **Small project (<$25K):** 5–8%
- **Mid-range ($25–100K):** 5–10%
- **Large (>$100K):** 8–12%

If general conditions on a $70K project is over $9,000 (13%+), the contractor is either inexperienced at scoping or quietly doubling up — billing supervision time inside both general conditions *and* the labor lines.

## Section 5 — Allowances

Allowances are placeholder dollars for items not yet selected. Common ones: tile, plumbing fixtures, lighting, hardware, countertops.

**Three rules for allowances:**

1. Every allowance must specify the unit (per sqft, per fixture) AND the total quantity, so you know the all-in dollar amount.
2. The allowance dollar value should reflect the actual market for mid-range selections, not the bottom-tier minimum.
3. The contract must define how overages are billed — at cost, at cost plus markup %, or at retail.

**Allowance trap:** "Tile allowance: $6/sqft." If you have a 50 sqft backsplash, the allowance is $300. The cheapest acceptable subway tile is $4/sqft. The mid-range you actually want is $12/sqft. You're now $300 over the allowance, and the contractor charges you for the upgrade plus a 15–25% markup.

## Section 6 — Permits and Inspections

A clean quote names:
- Which permits will be pulled (electrical, plumbing, building, etc.)
- Who pulls them (the contractor — never you, no matter what they say)
- Whether permit costs are included in the bid price or billed separately
- The inspection schedule (rough-in, drywall close-up, final)

**Red flag:** "Permits as needed" or no mention at all. Unpermitted work voids homeowner's insurance, complicates future home sales, and exposes you to fines from the local building department — not the contractor.

## Section 7 — Payment Schedule

A fair payment schedule for a $25K–$100K residential remodel:

| Milestone | % |
|---|---|
| Contract signing | 10% |
| Materials delivered to site | 20% |
| Rough-in inspection passed | 20% |
| Drywall close-up complete | 20% |
| Substantial completion | 20% |
| Punch-list + lien releases | 10% |

If your contractor wants more than 15% upfront before swinging a hammer, you're financing their cash flow. That risk is yours, not theirs.

## Section 8 — Exclusions and Assumptions

This is the section homeowners skip — and the one that costs them the most.

**Exclusions** are things explicitly NOT in the bid. Examples that should make you nervous:
- "Asbestos abatement excluded" (1960s+ homes)
- "Knob-and-tube replacement excluded" (pre-1950s)
- "Structural repairs excluded if discovered during demo"
- "Surprises behind walls billed at T&M (time and materials)"

These can absolutely be legitimate — but they're also blank checks. Push to convert each exclusion to a documented contingency: *"If asbestos is discovered, contractor will provide a written change order with itemized abatement cost before proceeding."*

**Assumptions** are conditions the contractor is *assuming* are true. *"Bid assumes existing electrical panel has adequate capacity for new loads."* If the assumption turns out wrong, you pay the upgrade — usually at premium change-order rates.

## The 30-second self-check before you sign

1. ✅ Every dollar in the bid has a clear destination
2. ✅ A stranger reading this bid could know exactly what's getting built
3. ✅ Materials are specific enough that you could buy them yourself
4. ✅ Labor is broken out by trade
5. ✅ General conditions is between 5–12%
6. ✅ Allowances name quantity and market-realistic dollars
7. ✅ Payment schedule ties to milestones, not calendar dates
8. ✅ Exclusions become written contingencies

Five "yes" answers = decent contractor. Eight "yes" answers = sign. Less than five = renegotiate or walk.
